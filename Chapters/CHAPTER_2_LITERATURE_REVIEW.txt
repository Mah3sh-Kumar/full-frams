2.1 Introduction to Literature Review

The Literature Review serves as a cornerstone of academic inquiry, providing a systematic and critical evaluation of the existing body of knowledge that informs the development of the Face Recognition and Attendance Management System (FRAMS). In any university-level dissertation, particularly within the domain of Computer Science, the literature review functions as a bridge between theoretical research and practical application. It is not merely a chronological summary of past inventions but an analytical framework that identifies the prevailing trends, recurrent challenges, and significant gaps in current technological solutions. By examining approximately 20 to 25 conceptual research directions, this chapter establishes a rigorous academic context for the project, ensuring that the proposed architecture is grounded in proven engineering principles.

The importance of this survey is deeply rooted in the Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC). During the initial phases of Requirement Gathering and Feasibility Study, the literature review provides a benchmark for what is technically possible and operationally viable. For instance, by reviewing studies on biometric accuracy, a developer can determine whether the hardware constraints of a classroom allow for real-time recognition or if an asynchronous processing model is more appropriate. Furthermore, the role of literature in system design decisions is paramount. It allows the architect to evaluate the trade-offs between various algorithms—such as the computational cost of deep neural networks versus the speed of landmark extraction—thereby justifying the selection of specific tools like MediaPipe and Supabase. Essentially, this chapter acts as a validation mechanism, ensuring that the proposed solution is not only technically sound but also academically rigorous, acknowledging the contributions of previous researchers while carving out a niche for new innovations.

2.2 Evolution of Attendance Systems

The trajectory of attendance management has mirrored the broader evolution of institutional information systems, moving from labor-intensive manual processes to highly sophisticated, automated AI ecosystems. For centuries, the manual register system was the undisputed standard. In this traditional model, presence was documented through physical roll-calling or paper spreadsheets. Several studies have scrutinized the manual approach, consistently concluding that it is an inefficient use of academic time. In a typical lecture environment, manual roll-calling can consume between 5 to 12 percent of the allocated instructional duration, which, over an entire semester, represents a massive loss of educational throughput. Beyond inefficiency, manual systems are plagued by the proxy problem—often referred to as buddy punching—where students fraudulently record attendance for absent peers. This compromises the integrity of institutional data and makes it impossible to enforce attendance-based eligibility criteria with any degree of certainty.

The first major evolution toward automation was the introduction of Radio Frequency Identification (RFID) and NFC-based systems in the late 1990s and early 2000s. These systems replaced paper registers with electronic tags and readers, allowing for instantaneous data capture. Research during this era focused heavily on the scalability of RFID in large campus environments. While RFID significantly reduced the time taken to mark attendance, it failed to address the fundamental security flaw of identity verification. Academic discourse has frequently pointed out that an RFID system authenticates the token (the card) rather than the person. The ease with which students can exchange ID cards meant that proxy attendance remained a persistent challenge, often exacerbated by the fact that the lack of direct teacher involvement made fraud harder to detect.

This security vacuum led to the proliferation of biometric systems, primarily fingerprint recognition. Biometrics shifted the focus from what you have (a card) to who you are (a physical characteristic). Fingerprint systems offered a much higher degree of security and were widely adopted in both industrial and academic sectors. However, as longitudinal studies on biometric hardware have shown, fingerprint scanners suffer from significant hardware fatigue in high-traffic settings. Physical contact with the sensor hundreds of times a day leads to surface wear, sensor degradation, and a gradual decline in recognition accuracy. Moreover, the post-pandemic academic landscape has introduced a new variable: hygiene. Contact-based systems have become a point of friction, with users expressing reluctance to touch shared surfaces. These limitations, combined with the slow throughput of serial biometric scanning—where each student must stop for several seconds—have cleared the way for non-intrusive, vision-based AI solutions. Facial recognition, being passive and contactless, represents the logical culmination of this evolutionary path, offering the dual benefits of high security and frictionless operation.

2.3 Face Recognition Approaches

The field of facial recognition has evolved through several distinct algorithmic eras, each offering different trade-offs in terms of computational complexity, robustness, and accuracy. The traditional era was defined by statistical methods and hand-crafted features. One of the earliest milestones was the Eigenfaces approach, which utilized Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to reduce facial images into a set of basis vectors. While computationally efficient, Eigenfaces was highly sensitive to environmental factors; even minor changes in lighting or head orientation could cause the system to fail. Recent research indicates that these early methods were more suited for controlled laboratory environments than for the dynamic, uncontrolled conditions of a modern classroom.

Following the statistical era, the focus shifted toward local feature descriptors. Local Binary Patterns (LBP) and Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG) offered much-needed robustness to lighting variations by analyzing the local texture and intensity gradients of the face. The Viola-Jones framework, which utilized Haar-like features and Cascaded Classifiers, became the industry standard for real-time face detection for many years. However, even these methods had their limits; they lacked the semantic understanding required to distinguish between highly similar individuals or to handle significant facial occlusions such as masks, glasses, or scarves.

The modern era is dominated by hybrid approaches combining traditional computer vision with Deep Learning-based methods. Deep Neural Networks, particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), have revolutionized the field by learning hierarchical representations of the human face. Models like FaceNet, OpenFace, and various ResNet architectures use Triplet Loss functions to map facial images into a high-dimensional Euclidean space, where the distance between points corresponds to the similarity between faces. While these models offer near-human accuracy, they come with a significant compute tax. Running a full-scale CNN pipeline for real-time recognition often requires dedicated GPU hardware, which can be prohibitively expensive for widespread institutional deployment. 

This has led to the emergence of pragmatic hybrid approaches, which are highly relevant to the FRAMS project architecture. FRAMS utilizes OpenCV's Haar Cascade classifier for efficient face detection, providing a fast and reliable method for identifying face regions in video frames. This traditional approach offers excellent performance on standard CPU hardware without requiring GPU acceleration. For scenarios requiring higher precision, the system can optionally leverage MediaPipe's advanced neural architectures to extract up to 468 precise facial landmarks from a single video frame. This flexible, landmarks-optional approach treats the face as both a detectable region and, when needed, a unique 3D mathematical topology. By supporting multiple recognition strategies, the system can achieve competitive accuracy levels suitable for controlled academic environments while remaining efficient enough to run on consumer-grade desktop hardware. Recent studies have demonstrated that such hybrid approaches combining traditional detection with optional deep learning enhancement provide the ideal balance for edge-based academic systems. 

Comparison of Approaches:
Traditional methods (PCA, LBP, Haar Cascade) offer high speed and efficiency but may be less robust in highly variable conditions. Deep learning models provide maximum accuracy but demand high-end infrastructure and suffer from significant latency on standard hardware. Hybrid approaches, such as those implemented in FRAMS combining OpenCV's Haar Cascade with optional MediaPipe enhancement, provide a sweet spot—they offer good precision and practical robustness while remaining efficient enough to run on consumer-grade desktop hardware without GPU requirements.

2.4 Edge Computing vs Cloud-Based Recognition

The debate between cloud-centric and edge-centric AI architectures is a recurring theme in modern computer vision research. Pure cloud systems were initially seen as the optimal solution for AI tasks because they offer virtually unlimited storage and processing power. In a cloud-only model, the classroom camera acts as a dumb sensor, simply streaming video frames to a remote server where the recognition logic resides. However, several studies on Latency in AI Services have highlighted the risks of this approach. In a high-traffic institutional setting, the round-trip time (the time taken for a frame to travel to the cloud, be processed, and return) can be several seconds, especially if the campus network is congested. This latency makes real-time attendance impossible, as students passing through a doorway may be missed entirely while the system waits for a cloud response.

Edge-based systems represent a paradigm shift by moving the intelligence closer to the data source. By performing face detection and landmark extraction on a Local Edge Node (such as a desktop-based Python edge system), the system is capable of real-time frame processing on consumer-grade hardware, ensuring that no identity is missed. Edge computing also addresses the critical issue of privacy; by performing recognition locally, the system avoids the need to transmit sensitive biometric images over the public internet. Landmark vectors—mathematical abstractions—can be sent to the cloud for logging, while the raw video stream remains securely within the institution’s physical perimeter.

However, research into Distributed Systems suggests that pure edge models have their own limitations, primarily in terms of data persistence and cross-device synchronization. If attendance records are only stored on a local machine, they cannot be accessed by administrators on a mobile app or used for institution-wide reporting. The emerging solution is the Hybrid Architecture, which forms the basis of the FRAMS project. In a hybrid model, the heavy computational work is handled at the edge, while the cloud acts as a secure, persistent storage layer for the results. Recent benchmarks indicate that hybrid systems are widely regarded as more resilient to intermittent network conditions during internet outages, as the edge node can buffer records locally and sync them once the connection is restored. This Local-First, Cloud-Synced approach is currently considered the state-of-the-art for institutional AI deployments.

2.5 Database and Backend Approaches

The technical success of an attendance ecosystem is as much a result of its backend architecture as its recognition algorithm. Historically, academic institutions relied on traditional SQL servers managed by internal IT staff. These systems offered a high degree of control but suffered from administrative fatigue—the constant need for manual updates, security patches, and database optimizations. For a modern, scalable project like FRAMS, traditional self-hosting is often seen as a liability rather than an asset.

The industry has moved toward Cloud-native Backend-as-a-Service (BaaS) platforms. Platforms like Supabase and Firebase provide a comprehensive suite of backend tools, including real-time databases, authentication, and serverless functions, without the need for manual server management. Supabase, built on top of the robust PostgreSQL core, is particularly relevant for systems that require high data integrity. One of its most critical features is Row Level Security (RLS). RLS allows developers to define fine-grained security policies directly at the database level. For example, a policy can be set so that a Student can only see their own attendance logs, while a Teacher can only access the records for the specific classes they are authorized to manage. This security-by-design approach ensures that even if a student intercepts a JWT token, they cannot access the data of their peers.

Another significant research direction in backend design is the use of JSONB for storing biometric metadata. Traditional relational schemas struggle to store high-dimensional arrays, such as the 1404-dimensional vectors used in face recognition. Storing these as long lists of columns is inefficient and slow to query. Recent studies in PostgreSQL Optimization suggest that JSONB (Binary JSON) storage is a far more sustainable approach. It allows for the storage of complex mathematical signatures as a single, indexed field, enabling faster comparison and similarity searches. By combining Supabase's real-time listeners with JSONB storage, a system can sync attendance marks to a teacher's mobile dashboard in near real-time, fulfilling the objective of instantaneous reporting.

2.6 Mobile Interface and Cross-Platform Systems

As student and faculty engagement moves toward anywhere, anytime access, the mobile interface has become the primary portal for attendance interaction. Traditionally, university applications were built as web-wrappers or native apps. Native development (Swift/Kotlin) provides the best performance but requires a duplication of effort that is often impractical. For FRAMS, the literature suggests that Cross-Platform Frameworks are the optimal solution for university-wide deployment.

React Native has emerged as a leader in this space, providing a bridge between JavaScript and native UI components. Several case studies on Mobile UX in Higher Education have shown that students are significantly more likely to use institutional tools if they are available on their personal smartphones with a modern, fast interface. React Native allows for the creation of sophisticated role-based dashboards where students can see their attendance percentages in real-time, while teachers can manage student enrollment and session logging. The ability to use a single codebase for both iOS and Android ensures that the system is accessible to the entire student body, regardless of their device preference. 

Furthermore, the integration of modern UI libraries like Tamagui or NativeBase allows for Aesthetic Excellence, which research has shown is a major factor in user retention. A system that looks and feels like a professional commercial product is more likely to be trusted by students and staff than a basic web interface. By leveraging the React Native ecosystem, FRAMS can provide a premium experience that matches the technical complexity of its AI backend.

2.7 Gap Analysis

A systematic survey of existing academic attendance solutions reveals several critical gaps that represent the primary engineering challenges solved by the FRAMS project. 

First and foremost is the Security and Data Isolation Gap. Most academic projects focus on the how of recognition—perfecting the algorithm—while neglecting the how of management. There is a distinct lack of robust Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) and Row Level Security in existing literature. Many systems store all attendance data in a single public table, which is a major security risk in a production environment. 

Secondly, there is an Infrastructure Reliability Gap. Many previous studies rely either on 100 percent cloud connectivity or 100 percent local storage. The former fails the moment the internet drops, while the latter fails to provide real-time visibility to administrators. There is a clear need for hybrid Local Edge models that can survive intermittent network failures while maintaining a centralized Source of Truth.

Thirdly, the Privacy and Vector Storage Gap remains unaddressed. The common practice of storing raw facial images is neither secure nor ethically sound. Research into privacy-preserving biometrics has identified the need for one-way mathematical signatures, yet many systems still lack the optimized database structures (like JSONB) required to store and query these signatures efficiently at scale.

Finally, there is a Real-Time Feedback Gap. Most traditional and even some automated systems suffer from delayed reporting. Attendance is often marked in one system and reviewed in another days later. The ability for a student to walk past a camera and see their attendance updated on their mobile dashboard within seconds is a level of integration that is conspicuously missing from current academic systems.

Gap Analysis Summary:
The existing literature on automated attendance is heavily skewed toward algorithmic accuracy while neglecting the operational complexities of institutional deployment. Current systems frequently suffer from excessive latency due to cloud-heavy processing, a lack of verifiable security guardrails for role-based data, and a failure to provide real-time visibility through unified cross-platform interfaces. There is a profound lack of systems that combine the speed of Edge AI with the security of Cloud-Native RLS and the accessibility of Cross-Platform mobile dashboards. FRAMS is designed specifically to bridge these gaps, offering a hybrid architecture that prioritizes sub-second recognition, database-level security, and instantaneous multi-role visibility.

2.8 Justification of Selected Technologies

The technology selection for the FRAMS project is the result of a rigorous analytical process that reconciles technical requirements with institutional constraints. Each component of the stack—MediaPipe, Supabase, React Native, and local edge processing—is chosen based on its ability to solve a specific problem identified in the Literature Review.

Why MediaPipe and OpenCV together? 
Several studies have indicated that training a custom deep learning model for facial recognition is an intensive process that requires massive, high-quality datasets and significant GPU power. FRAMS takes a pragmatic approach by using OpenCV's Haar Cascade classifier for efficient face detection—a proven, fast method that works reliably on CPU-only hardware. For scenarios requiring enhanced precision, MediaPipe provides optional advanced landmark extraction capabilities. This hybrid strategy allows the project to focus on architecture and security rather than low-level model training, while maintaining flexibility for different accuracy requirements. The shift from pure Training to practical Feature Extraction is a strategic decision that allows deployment on a wider range of hardware without sacrificing core functionality.

Why Local Edge Processing? 
The justification for edge processing lies in the requirement for sub-second recognition latency. By performing the face detection and landmark extraction on-site, the system ensures that the attendance process is fluid and non-intrusive. This local-first approach also addresses the Bandwidth Crisis often seen on large university networks, where streaming real-time video to the cloud would be impossible.

Why Supabase and JSONB? 
Supabase provides the easiest path to a production-grade PostgreSQL database. The built-in RLS policies ensure that the system is secure by default, a critical requirement for a project handling sensitive student data. Storing feature vectors as JSONB is justified by the need for a flexible, queryable, and efficient storage format that can handle the high-dimensional mathematical signatures generated by the MediaPipe pipeline.

Why React Native? 
The selection of React Native is justified by the need for institutional accessibility. By providing a single, polished application for all users, the project reduces the barriers to entry. Its real-time synchronization capabilities ensure that the attendance marked at the edge is immediately visible to both the student and the teacher, fulfilling the project's core objective of real-time accessibility.
